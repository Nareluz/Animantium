2.1 · Cell Structure

Animal and plant cells are eukaryotic cells. They share several structures, including a nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, mitochondria and ribosomes. However, plant cells also have a cell wall, chloroplasts (in photosynthetic cells) and a large permanent vacuole.

Bacterial Cells

Bacterial cells are prokaryotic cells. Unlike animal and plant cells, they do not have a nucleus or membrane-bound organelles. Their genetic material is found freely in the cytoplasm.

Cell wall Provides structural support and protection. It is made of peptidoglycan.
Cell membrane Controls the movement of substances into and out of the cell.
Cytoplasm Contains enzymes and is where many chemical reactions take place.
Ribosomes Site of protein synthesis.
Circular DNA Contains the main genetic material of the bacterium and is not enclosed within a nucleus.
Plasmids Small, additional circular DNA molecules that carry extra genes.

The functions of each structure are explained in the tables above. You should be able to associate each structure with its function and recognise it in a diagram or image.

Plant, Animal and Bacterial Cells

The structures of plant, animal and bacterial cells should be recognisable in biological diagrams and images.

You should be able to identify:

  • Animal cells: nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria and ribosomes.
  • Plant cells: nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, cell wall, chloroplasts, mitochondria, ribosomes and vacuole.
  • Bacterial cells: cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, circular DNA and plasmids.

Cell Division and Specialized Cells

Cell Division

  • New cells are produced when existing cells divide. This process allows organisms to grow, repair damaged tissues and replace old or worn-out cells.

Specialized Cells

Specialized cells have particular structures and adaptations that enable them to perform specific functions.

Ciliated cells Ciliated cells have hair-like structures called cilia on their surface. The cilia beat to move mucus towards the throat. The mucus traps dust particles and microorganisms, helping prevent them from reaching the lungs.
Root hair cells Root hair cells have a long, hair-like extension that increases their surface area. This allows them to absorb water and mineral ions more efficiently.
Palisade mesophyll cells Palisade mesophyll cells contain many chloroplasts with chlorophyll to absorb light energy for photosynthesis. Their elongated shape allows them to be closely packed together near the upper surface of the leaf.
Neurones Neurones are specialized nerve cells that transmit electrical impulses around the body. They have a long axon that carries impulses over long distances and branched endings that allow connections with other cells. Some neurones also have a myelin sheath, which provides electrical insulation.
Red blood cells Red blood cells transport oxygen from the lungs to body tissues, where it is used in aerobic respiration. They contain haemoglobin, a protein that binds to oxygen. Their biconcave shape provides a large surface area, and the absence of a nucleus leaves more space for haemoglobin.
Sperm and egg cells (gametes) Sperm and egg cells are specialized reproductive cells called gametes. During fertilisation, a sperm cell fuses with an egg cell to form a zygote.
  • Sperm cells have a tail (flagellum) that allows them to swim towards the egg. They contain many mitochondria to provide energy for movement.
  • Egg cells are larger than sperm cells and contain nutrient-rich cytoplasm to support the early development of the embryo.

Organisation of Organisms

Living organisms have different levels of organisation, from individual cells to complete organisms.

Term Definition Example
Cell The basic structural and functional unit of a living organism. Muscle cell
Tissue A group of similar cells working together to perform a specific function. Muscle tissue
Organ A structure made of different tissues working together to perform a specific function. Heart
Organ system A group of organs working together to perform a particular function. Circulatory system
Organism An individual living thing. Human

2.2 · Size of Specimens

Magnification

Magnification describes how many times larger an image is compared with the actual specimen.

The formula is: Magnification = Image size / Actual size

Where:

  • Magnification (M): how many times larger the image is.
  • Image size (I): the measured size of the image.
  • Actual size (A): the real size of the specimen.

Magnification has no units because it is a ratio between two measurements.

Rearranging the Formula

The magnification formula can be rearranged to calculate either the magnification or the actual size of a specimen.

The three formulas: M = I / A I = M × A A = I / M
Example 1: Calculating magnification

A specimen has an image size of 30 mm and an actual size of 0.15 mm.

M = 30 / 0.15 = 200

Example 2: Calculating actual size

A specimen has an image size of 20 mm and a magnification of 100×.

A = 20 / 100 = 0.2 mm

Answer: 0.2 mm

Always make sure that image size and actual size are expressed in the same units before using the formula.

Unit Conversions

Biological specimens are often very small, so measurements may be expressed in millimetres (mm) or micrometres (μm).

The relationship between these units is:

1 mm = 1000 μm 1 μm = 0.001 mm

To convert millimetres into micrometres: multiply by 1000.

To convert micrometres into millimetres: divide by 1000.

Example

750 μm / 1000 = 0.75 mm
